# I. Introduction

Soil heavy-metal pollution is an escalating global environmental crisis that threatens agricultural sustainability, ecological equilibrium, and public health. Unlike organic pollutants, heavy metals such as Lead ($\text{Pb}$), Chromium ($\text{Cr}$), Cadmium ($\text{Cd}$), and Nickel ($\text{Ni}$) are non-biodegradable, bioaccumulative, and exhibit high toxicity even at trace concentrations [1], [2]. In India, rapid industrial expansion, unregulated effluent discharge from tanning and dyeing corridors, metal plating operations, and decades of intensive phosphatic fertilizer application have severely degraded soil quality across major river catchments [3], [4].

Despite substantial monitoring efforts by regulatory bodies such as the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and state agricultural universities, spatial soil assessment in India faces three fundamental technical and economic challenges:

1. **Spatial Autocorrelation and Overoptimistic Validation:** Standard machine learning workflows frequently evaluate models using random train/test splits or random cross-validation. In spatial domains, Tobler’s First Law of Geography dictates that nearby observations share similar environmental attributes. Random splitting inevitably causes spatial data leakage between training and test sets, inflating reported accuracy by $15\text{–}30\%$ and yielding poor out-of-region generalization [5], [6].
2. **The "Black-Box" Interpretability Deficit:** Although non-linear ensemble algorithms (e.g., Random Forests, XGBoost, LightGBM) routinely outperform classical linear spatial interpolation techniques such as Ordinary Kriging, their internal decision dynamics remain opaque. Regulators require defensible, interpretable insights to distinguish geogenic baseline weathering from anthropogenic industrial effluent deposition [7].
3. **Absence of Calibrated Uncertainty & Inefficient Field Sampling:** Standard ML predictors provide point estimates without uncertainty metrics. When resources for field chemical testing (ICP-MS, AAS) are constrained, random spatial sampling is economically wasteful. Environmental agencies need uncertainty-aware active learning mechanisms to intelligently allocate sampling resources toward regions of high environmental hazard and high model epistemic uncertainty [8].

To resolve these challenges, this study establishes a unified, reproducible framework evaluated in the agrarian-industrial landscapes of Tamil Nadu, India. The core contributions of this paper are fourfold:
- **Leak-Free Spatial Machine Learning:** We implement strictly isolated Spatial Block 5-Fold Cross-Validation across 35 environmental covariates, demonstrating true spatial generalization capabilities across independent geographic clusters.
- **Multi-Element Geochemical Feature Attribution:** We deploy TreeSHAP to quantify global and local interactions between in-situ soil properties (pH, clay, SOC), topography, multi-spectral Sentinel-2 satellite indices, and anthropogenic proximity predictors.
- **Inductive Conformal Uncertainty Quantification:** We integrate Split-Conformal Prediction to construct valid, distribution-free $90\%$ prediction intervals with empirical coverage validation.
- **Risk-Uncertainty-Density Hybrid Adaptive Sampling:** We propose and statistically validate a hybrid active learning sampling strategy that accelerates model learning curves, reducing required field soil sampling budgets by over $28\%$.
